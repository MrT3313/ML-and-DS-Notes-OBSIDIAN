## Thesis

Deployment reads as one act, getting a fitted model out of a notebook and behind an endpoint, and the chapter's real claim is that it is three decisions. When a prediction is computed: ahead of any request, in a batch, or on the request itself. Where it is computed: in a data centre, on the device, or in a browser tab. How much model is shipped: the whole fitted artifact, or a compressed one. All three trade the same three quantities, freshness, latency and cost. The four myths the chapter opens with make the same point from the other side, that deployment is ongoing and multiplied rather than done once: a shipped model rots, it has to be updated as often as the pipeline can manage rather than as often as anyone planned, it is one of many models rather than the only one, and scale is the ordinary case. What the chapter never states, and what the notes it produced add up to, is that each of the three choices moves the bottleneck rather than removing it. Batch prediction buys latency with staleness, an answer that is instant because it was computed hours before anyone asked. The edge buys network time with a model that has to fit the device's memory, its arithmetic and its battery. And compression buys size with accuracy, which is not spent evenly: the evidence gathered for pruning puts the loss on rare and atypical examples and on underrepresented subgroups, exactly where an aggregate score averages it away. Huyen's own summary of the chapter adds a forecast, that as hardware becomes more powerful and more optimized for machine learning, systems will move to making online predictions on the device. That is a 2022 forecast and not a finding, and read against the join above it is a bet that hardware will eventually absorb the bottleneck the other choices keep moving around. Three facts about this run rather than about the chapter belong here as well. `Machine Learning/Operations/` went from four notes to eleven and gained its first sub-folder, `Compression/`. For the first time most of what a chapter produced passed [[MLOps]]'s membership test, that a note is about a model already in production rather than a model being built: seven of the eight new notes did, where [[DMLS Ch06 Model Development and Offline Evaluation|chapter 6]]'s one candidate, experiment tracking, failed it. And one note went to Systems Architecture, because where any computation runs is not a question about models.

## Extracted

Serving, in `Operations/`, where a fitted model is asked for its answer and the cost of asking is the thing optimized:
- [[Model Inference]], new, a `concept`, and the note [[DMLS Ch01 Overview of Machine Learning Systems|chapter 1]] asked for by name. Its first paragraph separates the statistics homonym, where inference is using data to infer the distribution that generated it, which is the fitting half. It defines prediction latency as a distribution read at a percentile, $L_{(q)}$, and prediction throughput with the atomic against bulk relation $X_b = b/L_b$, both following scikit-learn 1.6's page on computational performance. It states the latency a caller feels as a sum of network, queue, feature and inference terms, with the network floor linked to [[Edge Computing]] and the tail arithmetic of Dean and Barroso, and it dates the runtimes that load a model onto a device or into a browser. It also says plainly that scikit-learn has no serving layer.
- [[Batch and Online Prediction]], new, a `concept` with a `## VS`, under [[Model Inference]]. It puts the three modes usually listed on two independent axes, when the prediction is computed and whether it reads a streaming feature, so streaming prediction is a special case of online prediction and one cell of the table is empty. It bounds the age of a batch prediction at $\Delta + R$ with mean $R + \Delta/2$, prices the wasted work as $(1-f)N$ with batch cheaper on compute exactly when $f > c_b/c_o$, and states online prediction as feasible exactly when $L_{(q)} \le$ budget. It keeps the book's asynchronous prediction, another name for batch prediction, apart from SageMaker's Asynchronous Inference, which is a queued online endpoint.

Compression, in `Operations/Compression/`, the first sub-folder `Operations/` has had. It is earned by five notes produced at once and placed by motive rather than mechanism: every technique takes a fitted model as its input and exists because that model has to be served somewhere cheaper or faster.
- [[Model Compression]], new, a `concept` and the umbrella, kept a `concept` rather than an `index` because it carries its own mathematics, which is the [[Ensemble Learning]] situation again. It holds the size $S = P \cdot b$ as [[Scalability]]'s floor evaluated at $k = 1$, the compression ratio whose factors multiply when two techniques attack different ones, a table of which factor each technique attacks, and the accuracy trade as a drop $\Delta \le \varepsilon_{\text{acc}}$ chosen in advance. The two routes the raw reading left as empty headings, compiling the model for its target and running it on faster hardware, are researched subsections (TVM with AutoTVM, and the first TPU). It also sets the book's four-technique list against the four categories of Cheng and colleagues (2017) and says where they cut differently.
- [[Low-Rank Factorization]], new, a `method`: the break-even rank below which factoring saves anything, the truncated SVD as the best factors by Eckart and Young in the Frobenius norm and Mirsky in the spectral norm, and compact convolutional filters argued out as a separate category rather than a kind of factorization, which corrects the raw reading.
- [[Knowledge Distillation]], new, a `method`: the tempered softmax, the $T^2$ factor derived and then checked numerically, the provenance from Buciluă and colleagues (2006) through Ba and Caruana (2014) to Hinton, Vinyals and Dean (2015), and MNIST and DistilBERT as worked instances.
- [[Model Pruning]], new, a `method`, separated from search pruning in its first paragraph. It carries the tree origin as cost-complexity pruning, the CSR storage inequality $d < v/(v+i)$ that turns "sparse takes less storage" into a condition, Optimal Brain Damage's saliency derived, the lottery ticket and "Rethinking" readings set side by side, and Hooker and colleagues on what a compressed model forgets.
- [[Model Quantization]], new, a `method`: the affine map with its error bound, post-training quantization, quantization-aware training and low-precision training separated where the raw reading ran them together, the $32/b$ ratio linked to [[Scalability]], and the hardware and runtime facts dated to September 2026.

Where computation runs, in `Knowledge/Systems Architecture/Hosting/`:
- [[Edge Computing]], new, a `concept` under [[Systems Architecture]], defined from Shi and colleagues (2016). Its `## VS` sets it against computing in a data centre on cost, availability, latency and data custody, with the GDPR stated as what it requires rather than as something on-device processing satisfies by itself, and its formal statement derives the fibre floor of about 1 ms of round trip per 100 km and the inequality for when the edge wins on latency.

The cross-domain boundary is drawn rather than assumed. Systems Architecture owns where computation runs, for any software, and the round-trip floor holds whatever is being computed. This domain owns what a device asks of a model, memory, integer-only arithmetic and battery, which is why that half lives in [[Model Inference]] under `### Where inference runs` and not in [[Edge Computing]]. The two link each other and neither restates the other.

## Open questions

So the raw notes do not discharge the debt. Research partly does: prediction latency and prediction throughput are now defined as quantities in [[Model Inference]], from scikit-learn's own documentation, which is the first time since [[DMLS Ch01 Overview of Machine Learning Systems|chapter 1]] raised them that anything in the vault owns either. The debt was paid by documentation rather than by the reading, and only its model half.

- `Machine Learning/Operations/` reported, and not reopened, for the seventh time.
