## Thesis

Chapter 1 ended by naming the object worth reasoning about, and it is the system rather than the model. Chapter 2 is what follows from that, in four steps that each rest on the one before. If the object is a system then what it is held to are requirements rather than a score, and there are four of them: reliability, scalability, maintainability and adaptability. Three of those are asked of any software system whatever it stores and however its behaviour was arrived at, and only the fourth needs a fitted model in the sentence, which is why three of them were written into the neighbouring architecture domain and the fourth was not. If the requirements are set by people, then what the thing is finally judged on is a quantity the business cares about rather than one the model reports, and the tie between the two is an empirical claim about one particular system rather than anything the fitting procedure supplies, which is why the experiment that tests it has to be run on live traffic and cannot be run anywhere else: every offline number is a function of the same held-out data, so an offline pipeline reporting green is entirely consistent with the business metric getting worse. If the requirements come from several parties at once then there is more than one objective, and how they are combined is an engineering decision with a price attached rather than a mathematical detail. Scalarizing before the fit buys one model and charges a refit for every reweighting; fitting one model per objective charges more models and leaves the weights free to move afterwards; the crossover between the two is arithmetic, and it does not support decoupling by default the way the chapter's own advice does. And if all of that holds then the work has no end state, which is the shape the chapter closes on: six phases whose sixth returns to the first, with the feedback running not from anywhere to anywhere but from exactly the two phases that learn something out of a running system to exactly the two that build one, so deployment is a pass-through and scoping is reached only by completing a turn. The task framing sitting in the middle of the chapter looks like a detour and is not one. Deciding whether a problem is classification or regression, and how many labels an instance carries and how many values each label takes, is the same act of fixing requirements one level down, since a house price quantized into buckets and a spam score cut at a threshold are the same data under two framings, and which framing applies is decided by the designer rather than found in the data.

## Open questions

- `adaptability` has no owner, and this is the sharp consequence of the split above. Three of the four requirements are notes in `Knowledge/Systems Architecture/`; the fourth is a row in [[Machine Learning Systems Design]] and the word itself is claimed by nothing. Part of it was settled while the notes were being written and is recorded rather than left open. The nearest general requirement is evolvability, one of the three design principles under [[Maintainability]], and adaptability is strictly broader than it in two directions. It adds a shifting data distribution as a driver, rather than only changing requirements, and that half cannot be stated without a fitted model in the sentence, which is [[Model Rot]] under its own name with [[Continual Learning]] as the standing response. And it adds updating without interrupting service, which in the general scheme is an operability and deployment property rather than an evolvability one. What is left open is whether the word earns a note of its own or stays a row, and the trigger is [[DDIA]] chapter 2, "Defining Nonfunctional Requirements", which is the next chapter of that book.
- The three requirement notes were written into a neighbouring domain from a machine learning chapter, ahead of the book that owns them, and the cost runs both ways. What is bought is that the vocabulary is on disk where the membership test says it belongs, so [[Machine Learning Systems Design]] can state what a learned system adds to each requirement instead of restating each requirement, and nothing had to be filed in machine learning that is true of any service. What is paid is that three notes were written from one book's treatment of a subject another book is named for at chapter length, so their emphasis is this chapter's rather than that one's. That price is the ordinary one this vault pays for a second source: [[DDIA]] chapter 2 is named for exactly this trio, so when it arrives it amends three notes rather than creating them, which is the cheaper outcome. The alternative, writing all four requirements here in `Knowledge/Machine Learning/`, would have been cheaper today and wrong then, since the same chapter would have had to move three notes across a domain boundary before it could touch them. One smaller risk is worth recording separately: the folder is called `Requirements/`, and a chapter titled "Defining Nonfunctional Requirements" may well argue that the honest name is `Nonfunctional Requirements/`, which is a rename plus the prose in [[Systems Architecture]] that names the folder in words.
- A/B testing is used and not owned. It is glossed in one sentence inside [[Business Objective]], as a controlled online experiment splitting live traffic between two variants and comparing them on a metric fixed in advance, and it appears nowhere else in the vault. That gloss is enough to keep the sentence from being empty and nowhere near enough to be a note: how traffic is split, how long a test has to run before its result means anything, and how a result is read without fooling yourself are all missing, and all three are DMLS chapter 9's, "Continual Learning and Test in Production". Until then the note that most needs the mechanism, whose whole argument is that the offline-to-online implication has to be tested rather than assumed, cannot say how the test is run.
- `inductive bias` is glossed and not owned, inside [[Unreasonable Effectiveness of Data]], where it is what the Mind side of the argument actually names. The trap that comes with it is a vocabulary trap rather than a technical one, and it is worse than the fairness case chapter 1 recorded, because the collision is inside this domain rather than across it: `bias` in [[Bias-Variance Tradeoff]] is a precise statistical quantity in a decomposition of expected error, and it is already load-bearing in several notes. Any future note on inductive bias has to be qualified against that reading in its first sentence, the way the amendment already is. DMLS chapter 6, "Model Development and Offline Evaluation", is the likeliest trigger.
- `Machine Learning/Operations/` is reported on and not reopened. [[DMLS Ch01 Overview of Machine Learning Systems]] recorded the standing position, that the fold of MLOps into Machine Learning holds and nothing moves, with two triggers for reopening: the folder passing 25 notes, or DMLS chapter 10, "Infrastructure and Tooling for MLOps", arriving. The folder was listed while writing this note and holds four files, [[MLOps]] as its index plus [[Production Machine Learning]], [[Model Rot]] and [[Continual Learning]], which is exactly what it held after chapter 1. This chapter added nothing to it and took nothing out. Neither trigger fired, and the question stays where chapter 1 left it.
- Latency and throughput are still unowned, and this chapter does not settle it. Chapter 1 left them open and said they are owed by the first chapter that states one of them precisely enough to check a claim against. The raw reading notes for this chapter were searched for both words and contain neither, so nothing here discharges the debt. One thing did change and it moves the wrong way: [[Scalability]] now uses `throughput`, as the performance measure a batch system's coping is read on against response time for an online one, in a note whose only source is this chapter. So the vault has added a use of an undefined term on the authority of a chapter that never says it. That makes the term more load-bearing than it was without making it any better defined, and [[DDIA]] chapter 2 is where both words are expected.
- The vault now carries diagrams and has no convention for them. Two mermaid blocks landed in this run, the six-phase loop in [[Machine Learning Project Lifecycle]] and the task-type tree in [[Classification]], and before it the only fenced languages anywhere under `Knowledge/` were `python` and `base`. Nothing in [[Quality Bar]] says when a diagram earns its place, what it is allowed to assert that prose is not, or whether it counts toward any section's requirement. One part of that is already decided by the rules that exist: a diagram plainly does not count as `## Formal statement` content, because a picture cannot be found false, which is exactly why the lifecycle note draws the loop and then states its adjacency in prose as a directed graph with the back edges enumerated. What is undecided is everything else, including whether a diagram that is not backed by prose is a defect. This is a question about this vault rather than about the chapter, so no chapter settles it and it stays open until somebody writes the rule.
- The data science hierarchy of needs was declined a note, and the reasons should survive the decision. Only its precedence claim is kept, inside [[Machine Learning Project Lifecycle]], that nothing at the learning level is reachable until the layers under it are in place. Two things argued against a note. Its lower layers are `Knowledge/Data Systems/` material, already held by [[Data Engineering]], [[Extract-Transform-Load]] and the stores under [[Data Systems]], so a note here would have been a second home for them. And it was published as a blog post, Monica Rogati's "The AI Hierarchy of Needs" on Hacker Noon in June 2017, which is weak evidence for anything structural. One thing could not be verified and is recorded as unverified rather than asserted: the six level labels that appear in the reading notes appear to live only inside the article's pyramid image and not in its prose, which walks through eight stages in different words, so the vault is quoting a picture. Settling that means reading the article against the image.
- A coverage check against the chapter's own section list, of the kind that found chapter 1 never reached "Machine Learning Use Cases". It could be performed, and the result is clean. The printed table of contents, taken from the publisher's own front-matter preview of ISBN 978-1-098-10796-3 and confirmed against a library catalogue record, gives five substantive sections plus a summary: "Business and ML Objectives", "Requirements for ML Systems" with subsections Reliability, Scalability, Maintainability and Adaptability, "Iterative Process", "Framing ML Problems" with subsections Types of ML Tasks and Objective Functions, "Mind Versus Data", and "Summary". Every one of the five reached a note, and the requirements subsections reached one note each. Two differences from the printed chapter are in the reading rather than in the extraction: the reading notes put requirements before objectives, reversing the book's order, and they promote "Objective Functions" from a subsection of framing to a section of its own. Neither changes what was extracted. The check cannot go below two levels, because the printed table of contents stops there, so whether any third-level heading inside "Types of ML Tasks" produced nothing is not answerable from a primary source; the one third-level head confirmed from the preview's body text, "Decoupling objectives", did produce a note.
- The 16 GB figure could not be verified and is not in any note as a bare number. The reading notes claim a 100 million parameter network needs 16 GB of RAM, against 1 GB for a logistic regression model. Two independent research passes in this run found it absent from the chapter's available drafts, and the arithmetic does not reach it: 100 million parameters at four bytes each is about 0.4 GB as fp32 weights and about 1.6 GB carrying full Adam training state, so 16 GB implies roughly 160 bytes per parameter and is defensible only as a whole training footprint with a batch size stated alongside it, since the remainder is activation memory that scales with batch size, input length and depth rather than with the parameter count. [[Scalability]] therefore carries the formula and the two derived figures instead of the number. What would settle it is a page check against a physical copy of the book.
- Hierarchy-aware evaluation has no owner, which this run created the need for. [[Hierarchical Classification]] states that a plain accuracy score on the leaves treats a neighbouring-leaf error and a wrong-subtree error as the same miss, and that the two are not the same mistake, since an error high in the tree is unrecoverable and an error at the last level costs one sibling confusion. Every metric note in the vault is flat: the whole of `Objectives/Metrics/` reads a [[Confusion Matrix]] that knows nothing about a label hierarchy. So the note makes a claim about which errors matter more and there is no measure written down that can express it. DMLS chapter 6 is the likeliest trigger, being the offline evaluation chapter, and what is owed is a measure that reads the tree rather than another ratio of the same cells.
- Versioning and reproducibility are asked for by two of the four requirements and owned by neither. [[Maintainability]]'s machine learning row says what is versioned is not only the source, and [[Scalability]] says artifact management needs automation from the first model in production rather than the many-th. [[Machine Learning System]] holds the nearest thing to an answer, which is the claim that deployed behaviour is a joint function of code, data and the fitted artifact, so each has to be pinned separately. What is missing is the practice: how a training run is recorded so that it can be repeated, where experiment tracking sits, and what a model registry is. DMLS chapter 6 owes the experiment half and chapter 10, "Infrastructure and Tooling for MLOps", owes the registry half.

Settled here, having been asked earlier:

- [[HOML Ch02 End-to-End Machine Learning Project]] asked two things in one bullet, framing the business objective having opened that chapter and produced no note, and the ML project checklist having done the same: is the checklist a note of its own under `__Meta/`, or is a chapter note already doing that job? **Settled**, and the answer is a split rather than either option offered. The business objective now has a note, [[Business Objective]], which is more than a restatement of the step, since it separates three quantities that chapter had flattened into one and states the order-preservation assumption joining the offline measure to the business metric, along with the failure that follows when the assumption does not hold. The checklist question resolves as neither of the two options. The *cycle* earns a note, [[Machine Learning Project Lifecycle]], because it makes a structural claim that can be checked, that the feedback edges are exactly the complete bipartite connection from the two phases that learn to the two that build, and a checklist makes no claim of that kind. The per-project checklist does not earn one, because a chapter note is already the record of what one project actually did, in order, and a `__Meta/` file listing the same steps in the abstract would be that record with the evidence removed. So the answer to "is the chapter note already doing that job" is yes for the checklist and no for the cycle.
- [[DMLS Ch01 Overview of Machine Learning Systems]] asked whether `Machine Learning/Operations/` can carry this book. **Reported, not settled and not reopened.** The folder still holds four notes and neither of the two triggers chapter 1 named has fired. The count is unchanged because this chapter's material went to `_Foundations/`, `Objectives/`, `Tasks/Classification/` and the neighbouring architecture domain instead, which is itself mild evidence for the standing position: a chapter about designing systems added nothing to the folder about running them.
- [[DMLS Ch01 Overview of Machine Learning Systems]] asked for latency and throughput, from the computational priority row of its research against production comparison. **Not settled.** This chapter mentions neither word, checked against the raw reading notes, and the open question above records the one thing that did change, which is that [[Scalability]] now uses `throughput` without defining it.
- [[DMLS Ch01 Overview of Machine Learning Systems]] recorded that this vault uses "MLOps" more narrowly than the literature does, and left the gap on the record with chapter 10 as what would settle it. **Partly relieved, not settled.** The wide reading now has a name and a note of its own, [[Machine Learning Systems Design]], which is the view spanning both halves of the subject, so [[MLOps]] no longer has to be the vault's only account of that span and the narrow boundary costs a reader less than it did. What is unchanged is the boundary itself and the reason for it, and chapter 10 still decides whether it is redrawn.
- [[DMLS Ch01 Overview of Machine Learning Systems]] left [[Continual Learning]]'s placement in `Operations/` to be tested by DMLS chapter 9, on the question of whether it is a production practice or a fitting regime. **Corroborated, not settled.** This chapter groups it with monitoring as one phase of the loop, phase 5, "Monitoring and Continual Learning", which puts it beside watching a live system rather than beside how a gradient is computed. That is consistent with the current placement and is not what chapter 1 asked for, since a phase name is weaker evidence than a chapter-length treatment. Chapter 9 still decides.
- [[DDIA Ch01 Trade-Offs in Data Systems Architecture]] recorded that two domains carry an `Operations/` folder holding one note each, [[Data Engineering]] and [[DevOps]], making them the thinnest folders either domain has, and named chapter 2's operability material as what decides whether either grows. **Not settled, and the evidence has moved sideways.** Both folders still hold one note apiece. Operability arrived in this run, but as one of the three design principles inside [[Maintainability]], in a `Requirements/` folder that did not exist when the question was asked, so the material the question named as its decider has partly landed somewhere other than the folder it was expected to grow. When [[DDIA]] chapter 2 arrives it will be amending that note rather than filling `Operations/`, which makes the question less likely to be answered the way it was framed.
