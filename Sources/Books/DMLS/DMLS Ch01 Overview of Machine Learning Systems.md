## Thesis

Chapter 1 does two things, and the order they come in is the argument. First it puts a gate in front of the subject, a set of conditions a problem has to meet before machine learning is the right tool at all: five that are necessary, in the strong sense that failing any single one of them rules the approach out however well the problem scores on everything else, and four more that gate nothing and decide only whether the investment returns what it costs. What makes the list worth having rather than merely worth reciting is that it is written to let the answer be no, and each condition comes with the shape of its own failure attached. Successive rolls of a fair die carry no dependence of the outcome on anything observable, so there is nothing for a learner to find. A zip code fixes its state exactly, and a finite enumeration that is never wrong is not improved by approximating it. A problem whose answer is already determined by its inputs is not a prediction problem. A pattern that recurs a handful of times cannot amortise the data, the compute, the infrastructure and the people a learned solution charges up front. Read the other way the same list says where the escapes are, since the requirement that data exist is the one condition with recognised ways around it, and each of those routes satisfies it later rather than removing it. Then comes the reframing, which is what the remaining ten chapters are actually about: the object worth reasoning about is the system and not the model. A machine learning system is part code, part data, and part artifact fitted from the two, and nearly everything else in the chapter falls out of that one sentence. It is why the research setting and the production setting invert on almost every engineering priority, one agreed number on a fixed benchmark giving way to several parties who each want something different, a fit that finishes soon giving way to one answer returned to one caller who is waiting, and a dataset that holds still giving way to one that does not. And it is why pinning the code stops being enough to say what the deployed thing will do, since two deployments running byte-identical code behave differently the moment one of them was fitted on different rows.

## Open questions

- Latency and throughput are what this chapter's computational priority row actually turns on, research paying for a fit that finishes and production paying for an answer that comes back, and neither word has a note anywhere in the vault. Both are already in use without being defined: eleven notes across `Knowledge/Systems Architecture/` and `Knowledge/Data Systems/` use one or the other, and the two workload notes that lean on the words hardest define neither. They belong to Systems Architecture rather than here, since they are true of any service, and they are owed by the first chapter that states one of them precisely enough to check a claim against. [[DDIA Ch01 Trade-Offs in Data Systems Architecture]] asks for the same thing from the other side, recording that nothing in its 24 notes fixes a number for "online".
- Fairness and interpretability are two of the five rows of the research against production comparison and the chapter defines neither. [[Production Machine Learning]] glosses both from the research literature, which is enough to stop the cells being empty and not enough to be a note: fairness has several formalizations that are provably incompatible outside degenerate cases, and interpretability is not one notion but several that have to be named before the word means anything. Both are owed to DMLS chapter 11, "The Human Side of Machine Learning". One trap belongs with that and is a vocabulary trap rather than a technical one: everywhere else in the vault "fair" is the everyday word, a fair die, a fair draw, a fair comparison between a penalized model and an unpenalized one, so the algorithmic sense is new vocabulary here and any future note has to be qualified against the reading that is already in use.
- Transfer learning is described in this chapter and never named. It is the mechanism behind the data escape, a system answering for a task it was not trained on because it was trained on related ones, and both [[Zero-Shot Learning]] and [[Few-Shot Learning]] settle their relation to it in prose, each as the limiting or near-limiting case of it, without either claiming the name. Nothing owns the general case. DMLS chapter 4, "Training Data", is the trigger.
- [[Continual Learning]] is placed here as a production practice rather than a fitting regime, and it sits in `Operations/` on that reading, with the two questions held apart: how one update is computed against whether the updating keeps happening after deployment. DMLS chapter 9 is named for the term and is where that placement gets tested. If it treats continual learning as a regime rather than a practice, the note moves to `Paradigms/Training Regime/` and [[Online Learning]]'s alias list is re-cut at the same moment, since `streaming learning` is the weakest of its three aliases and only earns its place while online learning is the note a reader lands on when they mean a model being updated as data arrives.
- [[Model Rot]] holds five drift aliases in trust: `model drift`, `concept drift`, `data drift`, `model decay` and `performance decay`. Two of them name things its formal statement already distinguishes, drift in the inputs against drift in the relation between inputs and label, so they are candidates to become notes rather than redirects. DMLS chapter 8, "Data Distribution Shifts and Monitoring", is where `concept drift` and `data drift` are expected to earn that and move off the alias list.
- Whether `Machine Learning/Operations/` can carry this book. The folder went from one note to four with this chapter, and every one of the remaining ten chapters lands in it or next to it. The standing position is that the fold of MLOps into Machine Learning holds and nothing moves, because one chapter is thin evidence for a restructure and the cheap alternative, leaving the folders where they are and letting the folder grow, costs nothing today. The trigger to reopen is either that folder passing 25 notes, which would make it the largest in the domain, or DMLS chapter 10, "Infrastructure and Tooling for MLOps", arriving, since a chapter whose subject is infrastructure and tooling is the first that would plausibly need sub-folders under it. State the cost plainly, because it grows with every chapter extracted: a wikilink survives a folder move and prose naming the path in words does not, and four notes name that path in words today, [[Machine Learning]], [[Data Systems]], [[HOML Ch02 End-to-End Machine Learning Project]] and this one, each of them a string edit no link resolver will catch.
- This vault uses "MLOps" more narrowly than the literature does, and the gap is now on the record rather than hidden. The definition this chapter supplies is compatible with the narrow reading, since it glosses operationalizing as deploying, monitoring and maintaining, but that gloss defines the verb rather than fixing how far the term reaches. Every primary source checked reaches further: Google Cloud's practitioners guide puts ML development, data preparation and model training inside MLOps as the first of seven processes, its continuous delivery documentation begins its pipeline at data analysis, Sculley et al. (2015) frame the whole surrounding infrastructure as the subject, and CD4ML starts at dataset discovery and versioning. [[MLOps]] now states the narrow boundary as a filing decision rather than as consensus, which is honest but leaves a reader arriving from any of those sources expecting a folder wider than the one they find. What would settle it is DMLS chapter 10, "Infrastructure and Tooling for MLOps", which is where this book says at length what it thinks the term covers, and the answer decides whether the boundary is redrawn or the scope statement keeps arguing for the narrow one.
- "Inference" is used here for the forward pass at serving time, one input turned into one prediction by a model whose weights are already fixed, and nothing in the vault owns the word. [[Model]] owns what `predict` returns and says nothing about when it is called or what the call is named. It is owed to DMLS chapter 7, "Model Deployment and Prediction Service", and whatever note arrives has to separate the statistics homonym in the same breath, where inference is estimating parameters from data, which is the fitting half rather than the serving half and so means very nearly the opposite of what is meant here.
- Material in the chapter that never reached the raw notes. The chapter's own table of contents gives five sections and the reading notes cover three of them. "Machine Learning Use Cases", roughly pages 9 to 12, has no corresponding note at all, and neither does the anecdote the chapter opens with nor the idea of machine learning systems design that it hands forward to chapter 2. This is a gap in the reading rather than a gap in the chapter, which makes it the only question here that can be closed without waiting for a later chapter: the ten annotated industry case studies at this note's `url`, covering home-value prediction, streaming quality, ranking, fraud, routing, document text extraction, playlist generation and reply suggestion, are where to go back to.

Settled here, having been asked earlier:

- [[HOML Ch02 End-to-End Machine Learning Project]] asked what `Knowledge/Machine Learning/Operations/` needed before it counted as a real area, listing deployment targets, monitoring and alerting on live inputs, a retraining cadence, rollback, and where the confidence interval on a shipped model's error belongs. **Partly settled.** The count it carried, an index and a single concept note, has been struck from it: the folder holds four notes now, the setting itself is stated in [[Production Machine Learning]], and the retraining cadence now has a name and a research problem attached to it in [[Continual Learning]]. Deployment targets, monitoring and alerting, and rollback are all still absent, and DMLS chapters 7 and 8 are what owe them.
- [[DDIA Ch01 Trade-Offs in Data Systems Architecture]] recorded that the vocabulary for where anything at all runs now existed, and that what was still missing was the model-specific half, what serving a fitted model asks that serving an ordinary backend service does not. **Partly settled.** [[Production Machine Learning]] now states four of those asks: several parties with different requirements instead of one score to rank on, a single prediction returned to a caller who is blocked on it rather than a fit that finishes soon, data that keeps moving while the fitted artifact stays as it was, and fairness and interpretability becoming things somebody has to account for. The deployment mechanics are still missing, and DMLS chapter 7 now owes them alongside [[HOML]] chapter 19.
