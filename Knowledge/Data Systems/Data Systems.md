The machinery that stores, moves and serves data, kept as its own domain because every note in it would still be true if no model were ever fitted on anything it holds. That is the membership test, and it is the whole boundary: a note earns a place here when it is about where the data lives, how it gets there, who answers a query about it, and what breaks on the way. `Knowledge/Machine Learning/` owns what is learned from data; this domain owns the substrate that learning reads out of, which was built for reasons that have nothing to do with learning and would be built anyway. `Knowledge/MLOps/` owns operating a model that has shipped; this domain owns operating the services and the infrastructure underneath it, work that exists whether or not a model is among the things being operated. A note that only makes sense because there is a fitted model somewhere in the picture belongs to one of those two and not here. The domain is one chapter old. All twenty-four notes come from [[DDIA Ch01 Trade-Offs in Data Systems Architecture|DDIA chapter 1]], which surveys the architectural choices rather than the mechanisms, so what is on disk is the vocabulary and the shape of the trade-offs, and not yet how any single system works inside.

## Areas

- **`_Foundations/`** the vocabulary the rest of the domain is stated in. [[Data-Intensive Application]] is the axis the whole thing is organized around, an application whose primary difficulty is data management rather than arithmetic, with its four recurring challenges and the compute-intensive counterpart it is defined against. [[Node]] is the unit a distributed system is counted in, a process with its own state reaching its peers only by message, deliberately not a synonym for a machine. [[System of Record]] and [[Derived Data]] are the authority pair: the first holds the canonical copy and wins any disagreement by definition, the second is anything computable from it, redundant on purpose and rebuildable by rerunning the transformation, with the cache as the worked example of why a copy is allowed to exist only when a fallback path does.
- **`Workloads/`** the shape of the traffic a system is built to take, which is the split every later choice follows from. [[Online Transaction Processing]] is many small requests touching few records each, answered while somebody waits, and it carries the eight-row comparison table that the whole folder turns on. [[Online Analytical Processing]] is the other half, few queries each sweeping millions of historical records and returning a summary, including the product-analytics variant that tightens the latency target without changing the workload class. [[Point Query]] is the exact-match lookup by key that defines the first of those, and [[Transaction]] is the bracket an application draws around a group of reads and writes so the database's guarantees attach to the group rather than the statements. [[Hybrid Transactional-Analytical Processing]] is the attempt to put both workloads back in one system, and its internals mostly explain why they were separated.
- **`Analytics/`** where the copy goes once it has left the operational systems. [[Data Warehouse]] is the separate read-only store, conformed to one analysis schema on write and non-volatile so history accumulates instead of being overwritten, with a precise account of what it guarantees (isolation, one schema over many sources, rebuildability) and what it does not (freshness, authority, completeness). [[Data Lake]] is the same copy one step earlier, raw files with structure imposed at read time, defended by the sushi principle and undercut by the fact that nothing makes two files agree. [[Data Silo]] is the condition the warehouse exists to answer, one entity living in several systems under several identifiers with nothing holding the correspondence. [[Extract-Transform-Load]] is the running machinery of the copy, the only `method` note in the domain, carrying both letter orderings, what moves when the transform moves, and a failure list led by the upstream schema change nobody told the pipeline about.
- **`Deployment/`** who owns the machines and what that decides. [[Cloud Computing]] and [[Self-Hosting]] are one question asked from both sides, settled mostly by whether you already know how to operate the system and whether your load is predictable, with the cost stated as control given up rather than money saved. [[Cloud-Native Architecture]] is what a system looks like when it is designed for rented infrastructure instead of lifted onto it, and it is the most heavily audited note here: the CNCF definition is a property list rather than a technology list, and all four claimed advantages of ground-up design trace to papers by the engineers who built the system being measured. [[Object Storage]] is the lower-level service the higher-level ones layer on, a flat keyspace that trades most of the POSIX interface for durability nobody administers. [[Separation of Storage and Compute]] is the structural property the whole category is organized around, told apart from mere redundancy by the local disk against virtual disk comparison. [[Serverless]] pushes metered billing into code execution and is the one note in the domain with a real formula, because the bill is memory-time rather than time.
- **`Distribution/`** what happens once one machine is not enough. [[Distributed System]] is the general case and the longest note here, holding eight reasons a system ends up distributed, three costs it pays for it, and the COST measurements showing a hundred-core cluster losing to one laptop thread. [[Microservices]] is the deliberate cut, an architecture whose real subject is human coordination rather than machines, bought with a per-service operations bill. [[High-Performance Computing]] is the other tradition entirely, distributing one calculation rather than one service, and it is the useful contrast because it assumes a fast private interconnect and cooperative users, which is precisely the pair of assumptions cloud infrastructure refuses to make.
- **`Operations/`** who keeps the above running and who connects its two halves. [[DevOps]] is one team accountable for backend services and the data infrastructure under them, with the history, the practices, the distinction from site reliability engineering, and the observation that a cloud deployment does not remove operations so much as turn capacity planning into financial planning. [[Data Engineering]] is the role that sits between the operational and analytical sides, together with the analytics engineer who applies software discipline to the transformation code itself.

## What is missing

Almost all of the mechanism. This domain currently names parts and describes arrangements, and cannot yet say how a single one of the parts works. The honest list:

- **The building blocks themselves.** A data system is assembled out of five kinds of component: a database, a cache, a search index, stream processing and batch processing. Exactly one of the five has any treatment at all, and only as an illustration: the cache, inside [[Derived Data]], where it is the worked example of a copy that is allowed to be lost. Databases and search indexes arrive with DDIA chapters 3 and 4, batch processing with chapter 11, and stream processing with chapters 12 and 13. Until then the folders here describe how components are wired together without a note on any component.
- **The nonfunctional requirements.** Reliability, scalability, maintainability, availability, fault tolerance, latency and elasticity all appear in [[Distributed System]], every one of them as a reason to spread work across a network, and none of them anywhere as a topic. DDIA chapter 2, Defining Nonfunctional Requirements, owns the whole set. Until it lands, this domain can say why you would want a property and not what the property is, which means words like "scalable" and "available" are used across these notes and defined in none of them.
- **Two forward collisions that still need deciding.** [[Node]] holds the bare title today, and a decision tree node arrives with HOML chapter 6, so one of the two will have to be qualified before both can exist. The second collision is already settled by what is on disk rather than open: [[Model]] is the fitted hypothesis, so the data models of DDIA chapter 3 must take the full title Data Model and never the bare word.
- **A strain worth recording rather than acting on.** [[DevOps]] carries three things in one `concept` note: a practice with a datable origin, a definition of a job that used to be two separate jobs, and an economic observation about where the operations bill moves when somebody else owns the machines. The kind held, and the seam is visible anyway. The natural cut is a note of its own on the cost discipline that the capacity-planning-into-financial-planning shift turned into, which industry now calls FinOps and which [[DevOps]] names in passing. This is recorded as evidence and deliberately not acted on: [[Quality Bar]] says a new kind or a split is argued from accumulated records, and that the standing precedent for "not yet" is three.
- **`Operations/` is two notes and the thinnest folder here.** [[DevOps]] and [[Data Engineering]] are both about who does the work rather than about the work, which is a thin basis for a folder. Whether it survives as one, or grows into something larger once the operability material of DDIA chapter 2 lands, is open.
- **What could not be checked.** The second edition's reference list is published at `github.com/ept/ddia2-references` and the chapter text itself is paywalled, so several claims in these notes are recorded as the book's claims rather than verified against a primary source. The clearest case is [[Hybrid Transactional-Analytical Processing]], whose statement that the category does not replace a data warehouse could not be confirmed against Gartner, who coined the term in 2014, or against any vendor selling into it. The reasoning in that note is the vault's own and is defensible; the attribution is not.
- **The product lists go stale, and one already has.** [[Cloud-Native Architecture]] carries a table of twelve named systems, and by its own account four of those names were wrong or imprecise at the moment they were written down, through a renamed platform, a service tier listed as a peer of two products, a retired component and vendor prefixes that are not part of the names. That rate does not improve with age. The table is to be rechecked on every visit rather than trusted, and it is the wrong place to look for a current answer about any of the twelve.
