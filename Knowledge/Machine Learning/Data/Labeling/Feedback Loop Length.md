## Definition

For a task with natural ground truth, the feedback loop length is the time from a prediction being served to feedback on that prediction being available.

## Formal statement

It is a duration, so it has endpoints and units. For a single prediction served at $t_{\text{serve}}$ whose label becomes determinable at $t_{\text{label}}$,

$$\tau = t_{\text{label}} - t_{\text{serve}}$$

measured in units of time, from the moment the prediction is returned to the caller to the moment the ground truth for that specific prediction is observable by the system. Both ends are worth being exact about. The start is service, not the start of inference and not the start of the request, because the clock that matters begins when the prediction is out in the world being acted on. The end is observability by the system, not the moment the outcome occurred in the world: a chargeback that happened on Tuesday and reaches the processor's file on Friday has $t_{\text{label}}$ on Friday.

Because $\tau$ is defined per prediction, what a system has is a distribution over predictions rather than a single number. The mean is the wrong summary of it, since it is set by the fast majority, while everything downstream is gated by the slow tail: the honest quantity to plan a retraining cadence against is a high quantile of $\tau$, the horizon by which most of the labels for a given hour of traffic have actually landed.

The range is wide enough that the two ends are different engineering problems. Minutes for short loops, which is where recommendation sits, since a click either happens in the session or does not. Days to months for long loops, which is where fraud detection sits, because the ground truth is a dispute raised by a cardholder who has to notice the charge first.

### Choosing the cutoff, and what it costs

The reason this deserves a note rather than a definition is that $\tau$ is often not a property of the world at all. It is chosen. Many natural labels only become determinable by waiting for something not to happen: an unclicked recommendation is labelled negative once some window has passed, and the window is a decision, not an observation.

Let $F(\tau)$ be the fraction of instances that will eventually turn positive which have turned positive by time $\tau$, so $F$ is non-decreasing and $F(\infty) = 1$. Declaring a label at cutoff $\tau$ then mislabels a fraction

$$1 - F(\tau)$$

of the true positives as negatives. That is the speed against accuracy trade in one line: shortening the window buys fresher labels and pays in label error, and the payment is not random noise but a systematic error concentrated exactly on the instances that convert slowly. Choosing $\tau$ is choosing a point on $F$, and the two directions fail differently.

- **Too short.** $1 - F(\tau)$ is large, slow positives enter the training set as negatives, and the model learns to suppress precisely the items that would have been taken later. The suppression is self-confirming, because items the next model stops serving generate no positives at all, which pushes the measured $F$ down further. Worse, these errors are invisible in the ordinary way: since evaluation applies the same cutoff, the [[Confusion Matrix]] computed from those labels counts the mislabelled instances as true negatives, so nothing in the reported numbers shows the cost being paid.
- **Too long.** The labels are correct and stale. The model is being updated from outcomes generated by a world that has already moved, which is [[Model Rot]] proceeding during the wait, and the achievable update cadence collapses, since no system can be retrained from feedback faster than the feedback arrives.

## Where it is used

[[Natural Labels]] is what this is a property of: the contract there requires a bounded delay, and this note is the value of that bound. It is also what decides whether the arrangement is worth anything in practice, because a label that arrives after the decision it was supposed to inform has already been taken is a label nobody can act on.

[[Continual Learning]] is the practice whose achievable cadence it bounds, directly and from below: an update schedule shorter than the high quantile of $\tau$ is retraining on data most of whose labels have not landed yet, so the cadence is a consequence of this number rather than a free choice. [[Model Rot]] is what the delay lets accumulate undetected, since the performance decay that occurred during $\tau$ is decay nobody could have measured yet, and a long loop means the model has been wrong for that long before anyone could know. [[Hand Labeling]] is the escape hatch when $\tau$ is too long to work with, buying a fast label on a sample at the price of a human reading it.

The monitoring half of this, detecting decay from the same delayed signal and working out how long you must wait before a drop in measured performance is real rather than an artifact of labels that have not arrived, is a different subject and is not settled here; [[DMLS]] chapter 8 is where it belongs.
