## What it does and when

Knowledge distillation trains a small model, the **student**, to mimic a large model or an ensemble of models, the **teacher**, and the student is what gets deployed. "Distillation" here always means this model-to-model sense: one fitted model's behaviour is transferred into another. It is not dataset distillation, which synthesizes a small training set standing in for a large one and leaves the model out of it. [[Data Augmentation]] already names this technique in passing for generated text, where training a smaller language model on a larger one's output is one of the ordinary uses of synthetic data, and this note is the method behind that phrase.

What the student learns from is the teacher's output, not its weights. A teacher that says an image is a $2$ with probability $0.9$, a $3$ with probability $0.09$ and a $7$ with probability $0.01$ is telling the student something the hard label $2$ does not: which wrong answers are nearly right. That similarity structure is the "knowledge", and it lives in the small probabilities, which is why the method needs a way to make them loud enough to learn from (the temperature below). Because only outputs cross over, the transfer is the opposite of [[Transfer Learning]], where the source model's learned representation is reused on a new task and its predictions are thrown away; here the task stays the same and only the predictions are kept.

Reach for it when the model that scores best is too slow or too large to serve and a smaller one trained on the labels alone scores clearly worse. The large model is then worth more as a labeller than as a server. It sits among the [[Model Compression]] techniques with a difference in kind: [[Model Pruning]], [[Model Quantization]] and [[Low-Rank Factorization]] transform the weights of the model you already have, while distillation fits a new model whose architecture you choose freely, so it composes with the others (a distilled student can then be pruned or quantized). The standing reason the teacher is large in the first place is [[Ensemble Learning]]: an ensemble of $M$ members costs $M$ times the prediction latency, which [[Bagging]] lists as the price of its accuracy, and distillation is how that accuracy is bought back into one model.

### Who did what

The idea predates the name. Buciluă, Caruana and Niculescu-Mizil ("Model Compression", KDD 2006) compressed ensembles built by ensemble selection, whose libraries mixed SVMs, bagged and boosted trees, random forests, neural nets, logistic regression, k-nearest neighbours and naive Bayes, into a single small neural network. Their mechanism was the transfer set: they used the ensemble to label a large amount of unlabeled or synthetic data (generated by a procedure of theirs called MUNGE when real unlabeled data was scarce) and fitted the network to that, reporting networks a thousand times smaller and faster than the ensembles with nearly the same performance. Ba and Caruana ("Do Deep Nets Really Need to be Deep?", NeurIPS 2014) fitted students to the teacher's logits by squared error. Hinton, Vinyals and Dean ("Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network", 2015) named the method distillation, introduced the temperature, and gave the two-term loss below; they credit Caruana and collaborators for pioneering the strategy.

The claim that the method works regardless of how different the teacher and student architectures are holds for the loss itself, since the loss only ever reads the teacher's outputs on the transfer set, and Buciluă's heterogeneous ensembles compressed into a neural net are the strongest evidence for it. It needs two qualifications. The capacity gap matters: Cho and Hariharan (ICCV 2019) found that more accurate and larger teachers often make worse teachers for a small student, and Mirzadeh and co-authors (AAAI 2020) that student performance degrades when the gap is large, which they bridge with an intermediate "teacher assistant". And the best-known recipes do lean on shared architecture: DistilBERT initializes its student by taking one layer out of every two from the teacher, which is only possible because the hidden sizes match, and its ablation loses $3.69$ GLUE macro-score points when that initialization is replaced by random weights.

The claim that distillation depends on having a teacher, and that without one you must first build and train the teacher, is true of distillation used for compression, where the whole point is a larger model you already have. It is an overstatement for distillation in general. Furlanello and co-authors ("Born Again Neural Networks", ICML 2018) distill into students parameterized identically to their teachers and report that the students outperform the teachers, so the teacher need not be larger. Zhang and co-authors ("Be Your Own Teacher", ICCV 2019) distill the deeper sections of one network into its shallower sections within a single training run, with an average accuracy gain of $2.65$ percent. Yuan and co-authors (CVPR 2020) argue that distillation acts as a learned form of label smoothing and propose teacher-free distillation, where the student learns from itself or from a hand-designed distribution, reporting results comparable to distillation from a stronger teacher. A separate line removes the other dependency, on the teacher's training data rather than on the teacher: data-free distillation (Lopes, Fenu and Starner 2017; Chen and co-authors, ICCV 2019) synthesizes the transfer set from the teacher itself, and still needs the teacher.

## Algorithm or formula

Write the student's logits for one instance as $z_1, \dots, z_K$ over $K$ classes and the teacher's as $v_1, \dots, v_K$. The softmax of [[Softmax Regression]] gains a temperature $T > 0$:

$$q_i^{(T)} = \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \exp(z_j / T)}, \qquad p_i^{(T)} = \frac{\exp(v_i / T)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \exp(v_j / T)}$$

At $T = 1$ this is the ordinary softmax. Raising $T$ divides every logit by the same amount, which shrinks the gaps between them and flattens the distribution toward uniform, so the teacher's small probabilities grow large enough to carry a gradient. The ordering of the classes never changes, so the teacher's $\arg\max$ is the same at every temperature.

The student is fitted to a weighted sum of two terms, with $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ the weight on imitation:

$$\mathcal{L} = (1 - \alpha)\, \mathrm{CE}\big(\mathbf{y},\, \mathbf{q}^{(1)}\big) \;+\; \alpha\, T^{2}\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\mathbf{p}^{(T)} \,\big\|\, \mathbf{q}^{(T)}\big)$$

The first term is the ordinary [[Log Loss]] against the true one-hot label $\mathbf{y}$, computed at $T = 1$. The second is the divergence between teacher and student at the raised temperature. Writing it as KL or as cross-entropy makes no difference to the fit, because

$$\mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{p} \,\|\, \mathbf{q}) = -\sum_i p_i \log q_i + \sum_i p_i \log p_i = \mathrm{CE}(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{q}) - H(\mathbf{p})$$

and the teacher's entropy $H(\mathbf{p})$ does not depend on the student's parameters, so both have the same gradient. After training, the student predicts at $T = 1$.

### Why the $T^{2}$

Differentiate the soft term (cross-entropy form, per instance) with respect to one student logit:

$$\frac{\partial\, \mathrm{CE}(\mathbf{p}^{(T)}, \mathbf{q}^{(T)})}{\partial z_i} = \frac{1}{T}\big(q_i^{(T)} - p_i^{(T)}\big)$$

When $T$ is large against the logits, $\exp(z_i/T) \approx 1 + z_i/T$, and if both sets of logits are zero-meaned per instance ($\sum_j z_j = \sum_j v_j = 0$) the denominators are both approximately $K$, so $q_i^{(T)} - p_i^{(T)} \approx (z_i - v_i)/(KT)$ and

$$\frac{\partial\, \mathrm{CE}}{\partial z_i} \approx \frac{1}{K T^{2}}\,(z_i - v_i)$$

Two things follow. The soft term's gradients shrink as $1/T^{2}$, so without a correction every increase in $T$ would quietly hand the loss over to the hard term, and tuning $T$ would silently retune $\alpha$ as well. Multiplying the soft term by $T^{2}$ cancels the shrinkage and keeps the two terms' contributions comparable as $T$ changes. And the right-hand side is the gradient of $\frac{1}{2K T^{2}}(z_i - v_i)^{2}$, so in the high-temperature limit distillation is squared-error matching of the logits, which is the Ba and Caruana method recovered as a special case. At moderate temperatures the student pays less attention to the teacher's very negative logits, which Hinton, Vinyals and Dean suggest may help when those logits are noise.

The cancellation is checkable. In PyTorch 2.14, on one batch of random logits, the gradient norm of the unscaled soft term with respect to the student logits was $0.162$, $0.0096$ and $0.00059$ at $T = 1, 4, 16$, and multiplying by $T^{2}$ gives $0.162$, $0.153$ and $0.150$: the unscaled term falls by a factor near $T^{2}$ and the scaled one stays put.

### The worked instances

**Hinton, Vinyals and Dean on MNIST.** A large network (two hidden layers of 1200 units, dropout, inputs jittered by up to two pixels) made $67$ test errors; a small one (two layers of 800 units, no regularization) trained on the labels made $146$. The same small network trained with the added task of matching the large one's soft targets at $T = 20$ made $74$, recovering $(146 - 74)/(146 - 67) = 0.91$ of the gap, and it learned the effect of the jitter although its transfer set contained no jittered images. With every $3$ removed from the transfer set, the student made $206$ errors, $133$ of them on the $1010$ test threes, and raising the learned bias of class $3$ by $3.5$ brought that to $109$ errors with $98.6$ percent of the threes correct, a digit it never saw. On speech recognition, more than $80$ percent of the frame-accuracy improvement a ten-model ensemble made over a single model was transferred to one distilled model.

**DistilBERT** (Sanh, Debut, Chaumond and Wolf, NeurIPS 2019 workshop on energy efficient machine learning) is the standard worked instance for language models. The student keeps BERT's general architecture with half the layers, is initialized from alternate teacher layers, and is trained during pre-training on a triple loss: the soft-target term above, the masked language modelling loss that plays the hard term's role, and a cosine loss aligning the student's hidden states with the teacher's. In the paper's words, it is possible "to reduce the size of a BERT model by 40%, while retaining 97% of its language understanding capabilities and being 60% faster". The figures behind the first two are $66$ million parameters against $110$ million, so $1 - 66/110 = 0.40$, and the GLUE score relative to BERT-base. The third is inference time for a full pass over the STS-B development set on CPU at batch size $1$, $410$ seconds against $668$, a throughput ratio of $668/410 = 1.63$ and a time reduction of $1 - 410/668 = 0.39$; "60% faster" is the first of those two readings.

## Hyperparameters

|---|---|---|---|---|
| temperature | $T$ | none in the method. $T = 1$ is the plain softmax; Hinton, Vinyals and Dean used $20$ on MNIST and tried $1, 2, 5, 10$ on speech; the Hugging Face DistilBERT script defaults to `--temperature 2.0` | flattens both distributions, so the student is taught the ranking among the wrong classes rather than only the top one; in the limit, logit matching. | imitation weight | $\alpha$ | none in the method. Hinton, Vinyals and Dean found a considerably lower weight on the hard term generally best, and used a relative weight of $0.5$ on it for speech; the Hugging Face script defaults to `--alpha_ce 0.5` on the soft term | moves the fit from the labels toward the teacher. At $\alpha = 0$ it is ordinary training and the teacher is ignored; at $\alpha = 1$ the labels are ignored and the student can do no better than copy the teacher's mistakes | tune jointly with $T$ on validation. With no labels in the transfer set, $\alpha = 1$ is forced |
| student capacity | | chosen by the deployment budget | the student can represent more of the teacher's function, up to the point where a larger student is no longer a compression | fix it from the latency and memory budget first. If the gap to the teacher is large, consider a less accurate or early-stopped teacher (Cho and Hariharan) or an intermediate teacher assistant (Mirzadeh and co-authors) rather than a bigger student |

Unlabeled production-like data is fine, since the teacher supplies the targets |

The student's initialization and [[Random Seed]] change the fitted student and are pinned rather than tuned; DistilBERT's layer-copying initialization is a design decision of that kind, and its measured value was the $3.69$ points above.

## Failure modes

- **The capacity gap is too wide.** Distilling a very large teacher into a very small student can give a worse student than distilling a smaller teacher into it, which Cho and Hariharan report as larger models often not making better teachers. The symptom is a student that trails a same-size model distilled from a modest teacher. Early-stopping the teacher or inserting a teacher assistant are the published repairs.
- **The transfer set is the training set and the student can already fit it.** Then the teacher adds little the labels did not. Measured here in scikit-learn 1.6 on the digits data (the code below), a depth-8 tree fitted to a 500-tree forest's probabilities on the training rows alone scored $0.830$ mean test accuracy over ten splits against $0.831$ for the same tree on the hard labels, no difference at all. Enlarging the transfer set with ten jittered copies of each training image labelled by the teacher moved the student to $0.889$. That is the Buciluă result reproduced at small scale: the gain came from the teacher labelling more of the space, not from the soft targets on their own.
- **The transfer set is missing part of the label space.** A class the transfer set never shows is learned only through the teacher's small probabilities on other classes, and its bias ends up wrong: the MNIST student with every $3$ removed got only $877$ of the $1010$ test threes right until its class-3 bias was raised by hand. A transfer set drawn from a different distribution than production has the same problem in a milder form.
- **Reading the student as a copy of the teacher.** Stanton and co-authors ("Does Knowledge Distillation Really Work?", NeurIPS 2021) found a large discrepancy between teacher and student predictive distributions even when the student had the capacity to match exactly, traced it to optimization difficulty, and found that closer matching did not always mean better student generalization. Evaluate the student as its own model on held-out data, including per slice ([[Slice-Based Evaluation]]), never by quoting the teacher's score.
- **Mis-scaling the soft term.** Dropping the $T^{2}$ factor means that raising $T$ also shrinks the soft term's share of the gradient by $1/T^{2}$, so a temperature sweep is really a sweep over $\alpha$ as well. In PyTorch, `F.kl_div` with its default `reduction="mean"` averages over classes as well as instances and does not return the KL divergence; the documentation says to use `reduction="batchmean"`. And leaving the student at the raised temperature at inference flattens its probabilities, which does not change the $\arg\max$ but corrupts anything read from them ([[Model Calibration]]).

## Implementation

**scikit-learn has no knowledge distillation API.** Nothing in scikit-learn 1.6 takes a teacher and a student. The honest nearest statement is the procedure with nothing to name it: fit a small estimator to a large one's `predict_proba` output, which is the $\alpha = 1$, $T = 1$ case of the loss above with squared error standing in for the divergence. A `DecisionTreeRegressor` accepts a two-dimensional target, so one shallow tree can regress all $K$ class probabilities at once.

scikit-learn 1.6 and NumPy 2.x:

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier, DecisionTreeRegressor

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=0)

# teacher: 500 trees, far too many nodes to ship
teacher = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=500, random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)

# transfer set: the training rows plus jittered copies, labelled by the teacher, not by hand
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X_transfer = np.vstack([X_train] + [np.clip(X_train + rng.normal(0, 2.0, X_train.shape), 0, 16)
                                    for _ in range(10)])
soft_targets = teacher.predict_proba(X_transfer)          # shape (n_transfer, 10), rows sum to 1

# student: one depth-8 tree regressing the whole probability vector
student = DecisionTreeRegressor(max_depth=8, random_state=0).fit(X_transfer, soft_targets)
y_student = teacher.classes_[student.predict(X_test).argmax(axis=1)]

# the same tree fitted on the hard labels, the thing distillation has to beat
plain = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=8, random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)

print(teacher.score(X_test, y_test), (y_student == y_test).mean(), plain.score(X_test, y_test))
```

Over ten splits the means were $0.977$ for the teacher, $0.889$ for the student and $0.831$ for the plain tree, with the student at $501$ nodes against the forest's $165{,}076$ on the split shown, a ratio of about $1/330$. A tree has no logits, so the temperature has no direct counterpart here; raising $p_i^{1/T}$ and renormalizing is the equivalent, since $\log p_i$ is a logit up to a constant, and with the training rows as the transfer set, $T = 1, 2, 4$ gave $0.830$, $0.835$ and $0.827$ over the same ten splits, nothing outside the split-to-split spread of about $0.01$ to $0.02$.

Where distillation is actually done is a deep learning framework with a hand-written loss. PyTorch 2.14:

```python
import torch.nn.functional as F

def distillation_loss(student_logits, teacher_logits, y, T=4.0, alpha=0.9):
    soft = F.kl_div(F.log_softmax(student_logits / T, dim=-1),       # input: student log-probabilities
                    F.log_softmax(teacher_logits / T, dim=-1),       # target: teacher, also in log space
                    reduction="batchmean", log_target=True) * T**2   # batchmean is the true KL; T**2 restores scale
    hard = F.cross_entropy(student_logits, y)                        # T = 1 against the labels
    return alpha * soft + (1 - alpha) * hard
```

The teacher's logits are computed under `torch.no_grad()` with the teacher in evaluation mode, and only the student's parameters go to the optimizer. `F.kl_div(input, target, ...)` takes the student as `input` in log-probabilities and the teacher as `target`, which is the reverse of the $\mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{p} \,\|\, \mathbf{q})$ reading order, and `log_target=True` lets the teacher stay in log space for numerical stability.

The DistilBERT recipe itself is Hugging Face's `distillation` research project, moved in 2025 from `examples/research_projects/distillation` in the `transformers` repository to the separate `huggingface/transformers-research-projects` repository. Its `distiller.py` implements the soft term as `nn.KLDivLoss(reduction="batchmean")` on the tempered log-softmax of the student against the tempered softmax of the teacher, multiplied by `self.temperature ** 2`, which is the loss above with the masked language modelling and cosine terms added under their own weights `alpha_mlm` and `alpha_cos`.
