How software systems are arranged: how many machines run them, who owns those machines, where the bytes sit relative to the code that reads them, and who keeps the arrangement alive. A note earns a place here when its definition holds for any software system regardless of what that system stores, which is the whole membership test and the whole boundary. Notes are sorted by what their definition requires and not by what their examples happen to be, because the evidence a definition is illustrated with is replaceable and the definition is not: a distributed system is several machines communicating over a network whatever travels between them, and an object store's interface contract is the same contract whether the objects are invoices, model checkpoints or video. `Knowledge/Data Systems/` owns everything whose definition names data: the shape of the traffic, the stores the copies land in, the transaction bracket, the pipelines that move them. This domain owns the substrate all of that is assembled on, which was built for reasons that have nothing to do with what is being stored and would be built anyway. The domain now holds eighteen notes, from four chapters of two books. Eleven come from [[DDIA Ch01 Trade-Offs in Data Systems Architecture|DDIA chapter 1]], which surveys architectural choices rather than mechanisms, so that part of what is on disk is the vocabulary and the shape of the trade-offs, and not yet how any single system works inside. Three are `Requirements/`, which arrived from [[DMLS Ch02 Introduction to Machine Learning Systems Design|DMLS chapter 2]] reaching for vocabulary this domain owns: reliability, scalability and maintainability are asked of any software system whatever it stores, so they are here by the membership test working as intended rather than as an exception to it. Those three state the general requirement, and what a learned system adds to each one is stated in [[Machine Learning Systems Design]], on the other side of the boundary. Three more are in `Distribution/` and arrived by the same route, from [[DMLS Ch03 Data Engineering Fundamentals|DMLS chapter 3]]: a chapter about data produced three notes about processes, since how two processes that share no memory pass data to each other holds whatever it is they are passing. The last, [[Edge Computing]] in `Hosting/`, came the same way from [[DMLS Ch07 Model Deployment and Prediction Service|DMLS chapter 7]]: a chapter about serving models had to ask where any computation runs, and the answer is bounded by the speed of light in fibre whatever is being computed.

## Areas

- **`Requirements/`** what is asked of a system before anything about its arrangement is settled. [[Reliability]] is the requirement that the correct function continue at the desired level of performance in the face of adversity, and its content is the pair of terms the ordinary word "failure" collapses: a fault is one component deviating from its specification, a failure is the system as a whole no longer serving its user, and fault tolerance is the property of keeping the first from becoming the second. From that follows the clause that makes a reliability claim checkable at all, that no system tolerates every possible fault, so a claim naming no fault class cannot be found false and is not a claim. It carries the arithmetic that turns a fault class into a rate, $N \cdot r$ failures a year, with Schroeder and Gibson's measured 2% to 4% annual disk replacement rates against a datasheet figure of at most 0.88%, which puts a ten thousand disk fleet at 200 to 400 replacements a year, and Oppenheimer, Ganapathi and Patterson's finding that operator error, mostly misconfiguration, was the largest single cause of failure in two of the three large internet services they studied. [[Scalability]] refuses to be a label a system has or lacks: it is the question of what the options are if the system grows in one named way, made answerable by a load parameter and a performance measure and the two questions asked of them, what happens to performance when resources are held fixed and what resources hold performance fixed. It states horizontal scaling against vertical scaling as the two ways to answer the second, only one of which is bounded by the largest machine anyone builds, and it carries two floors as formulas rather than anecdotes, $P \cdot b \cdot k$ for the memory one copy of a model occupies, which puts a hundred million parameters at 0.4 GB served and 1.6 GB trained and leaves the rest of any larger figure to activation memory that no one can compute without a batch size, and $N / 86400$ for traffic quoted per day and paid per second. [[Maintainability]] is the requirement that whoever works on the system later can, and it splits into operability, simplicity and evolvability, three things to build for that are traded against one another rather than three words for one. It sharpens the middle one with Brooks's accidental complexity, complexity not inherent in the problem as the users see it but arising only from the implementation, and with the test that definition yields: whether a given piece of complexity would survive a reimplementation against the same requirements. Glass's 40% to 80% of whole-life cost falling after the first release is why the requirement outranks the build.
- **`Distribution/`** what happens once one machine is not enough. [[Distributed System]] is the general case and the longest note here, carrying the system model every protocol is proved against (no shared memory, no shared clock, messages liable to be delayed, dropped, duplicated or reordered, a crashed node indistinguishable from a slow one), eight reasons a system ends up distributed, three costs it pays for it, and the COST measurements of McSherry, Isard and Murray: twenty PageRank iterations over the twitter_rv graph took 275 seconds in a single laptop thread against 857 seconds for Spark on 128 cores, and on label propagation that same 128-core cluster was more than eleven times slower than the one thread. [[Node]] is the unit such a system is counted in, deliberately a process rather than a machine, with a four-part test (private state no peer reads directly, its own clock, reachable only by message, fails on its own) that makes three database processes on one laptop three nodes and a replication factor a well-defined number. [[Microservices]] is the deliberate cut, an architectural contract of one purpose per service, an API as the only way in, and one owning team, whose real subject is human coordination rather than machines: it bets that network boundaries between components cost less than coordination across a single codebase, and it pays for the bet with deployment, logging and monitoring multiplied by the number of services. [[High-Performance Computing]] is the other tradition entirely, distributing one calculation rather than one service, separated by three conditions that are each checkable against an installation: the job is a restartable batch checkpointed against a scheduler, memory is shared inside a node and crosses node boundaries only over a dedicated interconnect by remote direct memory access, and users trust each other far enough that kernel protections are bypassed and file system permissions are the privilege boundary. The last two are precisely the pair of assumptions cloud infrastructure refuses to make. [[Modes of Data Flow]] is the question every boundary those first three notes draw has to answer, since processes with no shared memory cannot hand over a pointer: the data passes through a store, through a request or through a broker, and the note settles the three against each other on five questions, what sits in the middle, whether the producer names the consumer, whether the consumer has to be running, where the data waits, and what the producer learns. It keeps the store mode in full, with both of the constraints that mode imposes, that both processes must be able to reach one database and that every value crosses it twice, and gives the other two modes notes of their own. [[Request-Driven Communication]] is the one where the caller names the callee, waits on the same connection and learns the outcome at the point in its own code where the call was made, and its contract's sharpest consequence is what the waiting costs once systems are assembled from it: availability along a chain of synchronous calls is the product $\prod_i a_i$ of the members' availabilities, so the chain is never more available than its worst member, and that on an independence assumption the note refuses to grant to services sharing a datacenter or a deployment pipeline. It also separates REST the architectural style, the six constraints of Fielding's 2000 dissertation, from REST the industry convention of an HTTP interface with resource-shaped URLs and JSON bodies, dating the split with Fielding's own 2008 complaint about it, and it files tRPC under RPC rather than beside it. [[Event-Driven Communication]] is that same contract with the availability clause dropped: the producer publishes to a topic the broker owns, the consumer need not be running, and the product above is broken by concentrating the availability requirement in the broker instead, which is why brokers replicate. The price is that failure moves off the call site, where somebody's code is waiting for it, to a lag measurement somebody has to have thought to monitor, and the promise is bounded by the retention window, a consumer out for $t$ losing nothing while $t \le R$ and losing the events of the first $t - R$ once it is not. It also declines the usual publish-subscribe against message-queue taxonomy of products, since Kafka's consumer groups select either semantics per subscription over one unchanged topic.
- **`Hosting/`** who owns the machines, where the computation runs, and what those two answers decide. [[Cloud Computing]] and [[Self-Hosting]] are one question asked from both sides, settled mostly by whether you already know how to operate this particular system and whether your load is predictable, with the cost stated as control given up rather than money saved: the first carries the control table that prices each consequence (ask the vendor for the missing feature, wait out an outage with no access to the failing machines, debug from outside on whichever signals are exposed, pay whatever leaving costs), plus the 2022 figures that put a rented 128-core bare machine at $1,318 a month against roughly $6,055 for the nearest AWS on-demand instance and about $40,000 to buy the equivalent outright. [[Self-Hosting]] states the same trade as the responsibilities a vendor never takes off you, one standing obligation per row: hardware and its depreciation, capacity sized for the peak in advance, upgrade timing and upgrade risk, durability proven by restore drills, and availability with somebody on call. [[Cloud-Native Architecture]] is what a system looks like when it is designed for rented infrastructure instead of lifted onto it, and it is the most heavily audited note here: CNCF's definition is a property list rather than a technology list, so containers, service meshes and microservices are typical ingredients and never membership, and all four claimed advantages of ground-up design trace to papers by the engineers who built the system being measured, with the one independent controlled study finding disaggregated storage alone costing 16.4x on reads and 17.9x on writes. [[Serverless]] pushes metered billing into code execution, and the bill is memory-time rather than time: duration rounded up to the millisecond and multiplied by allocated memory, plus a per-invocation charge, so idle genuinely costs nothing and very many short calls price quite differently from one long one. [[Edge Computing]] is the other axis, where the work runs rather than who runs the machines, and either answer combines with either ownership answer: it moves computation onto or near the device that produces the data, which buys operation with no network, a smaller custody footprint, and freedom from a round-trip floor of about 1 ms per 100 km of fibre, $2d / (2 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s})$, that no data centre can get under, and it wins on latency exactly when the slower device costs less extra compute time than that round trip saves.
- **`Storage/`** where the bytes sit relative to the code that reads them. [[Object Storage]] keeps whole files under names in a flat keyspace and trades most of the POSIX contract for durability nobody administers: no partial write, no general append, no atomic rename and no real directory tree on a general purpose S3 bucket, against eleven nines of designed annual durability, $10^{-11}$ expected loss per object-year, bought by replicating every object across at least three physically separated availability zones that the API never shows you. [[Separation of Storage and Compute]] is the structural property the whole category is organized around, told apart from mere redundancy by the local disk against virtual disk comparison: RAID keeps copies on one machine and loses every one of them with it, a local disk is an ephemeral cache that dies with the instance, and a virtual disk detaches and reattaches, survives a resize, and is replicated by the service that provides it. That separation costs roughly 30x the latency of a local read (about 500 microseconds across a datacenter against about 16 for an SSD) and buys an annual durability figure a local disk has no unit for, since its life ends when the instance does.
- **`Operations/`** who keeps all of the above running. [[DevOps]] is one team accountable for backend services and the infrastructure underneath them, dated to the first devopsdays in Ghent in 2009 and to Allspaw and Hammond's account of deploying Flickr more than ten times a day, and it carries three things at once: the practices the merge brings (automation replacing one-off manual work, ephemeral instances rather than long-lived servers, frequent updates, learning from incidents, and keeping what the organization knows as individual people leave), the distinction from site reliability engineering, which originated at Google in 2003 and is the narrower and more prescriptive of the two rather than a synonym, and the observation that a cloud deployment does not remove operations so much as change its shape, turning capacity planning into financial planning and performance optimization into cost optimization. The customer side of that substitution has since been named and organized as FinOps, a Linux Foundation project since 2020, built on the same move DevOps made with finance standing where operations stood.
