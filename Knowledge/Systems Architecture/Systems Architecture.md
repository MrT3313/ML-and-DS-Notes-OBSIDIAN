How software systems are arranged: how many machines run them, who owns those machines, where the bytes sit relative to the code that reads them, and who keeps the arrangement alive. A note earns a place here when its definition holds for any software system regardless of what that system stores, which is the whole membership test and the whole boundary. Notes are sorted by what their definition requires and not by what their examples happen to be, because the evidence a definition is illustrated with is replaceable and the definition is not: a distributed system is several machines communicating over a network whatever travels between them, and an object store's interface contract is the same contract whether the objects are invoices, model checkpoints or video. `Knowledge/Data Systems/` owns everything whose definition names data: the shape of the traffic, the stores the copies land in, the transaction bracket, the pipelines that move them. This domain owns the substrate all of that is assembled on, which was built for reasons that have nothing to do with what is being stored and would be built anyway. The domain is one chapter old. All eleven notes come from [[DDIA Ch01 Trade-Offs in Data Systems Architecture|DDIA chapter 1]], which surveys architectural choices rather than mechanisms, so what is on disk is the vocabulary and the shape of the trade-offs, and not yet how any single system works inside.

## Areas

- **`Distribution/`** what happens once one machine is not enough. [[Distributed System]] is the general case and the longest note here, carrying the system model every protocol is proved against (no shared memory, no shared clock, messages liable to be delayed, dropped, duplicated or reordered, a crashed node indistinguishable from a slow one), eight reasons a system ends up distributed, three costs it pays for it, and the COST measurements of McSherry, Isard and Murray: twenty PageRank iterations over the twitter_rv graph took 275 seconds in a single laptop thread against 857 seconds for Spark on 128 cores, and on label propagation that same 128-core cluster was more than eleven times slower than the one thread. [[Node]] is the unit such a system is counted in, deliberately a process rather than a machine, with a four-part test (private state no peer reads directly, its own clock, reachable only by message, fails on its own) that makes three database processes on one laptop three nodes and a replication factor a well-defined number. [[Microservices]] is the deliberate cut, an architectural contract of one purpose per service, an API as the only way in, and one owning team, whose real subject is human coordination rather than machines: it bets that network boundaries between components cost less than coordination across a single codebase, and it pays for the bet with deployment, logging and monitoring multiplied by the number of services. [[High-Performance Computing]] is the other tradition entirely, distributing one calculation rather than one service, separated by three conditions that are each checkable against an installation: the job is a restartable batch checkpointed against a scheduler, memory is shared inside a node and crosses node boundaries only over a dedicated interconnect by remote direct memory access, and users trust each other far enough that kernel protections are bypassed and file system permissions are the privilege boundary. The last two are precisely the pair of assumptions cloud infrastructure refuses to make.
- **`Hosting/`** who owns the machines and what that decides. [[Cloud Computing]] and [[Self-Hosting]] are one question asked from both sides, settled mostly by whether you already know how to operate this particular system and whether your load is predictable, with the cost stated as control given up rather than money saved: the first carries the control table that prices each consequence (ask the vendor for the missing feature, wait out an outage with no access to the failing machines, debug from outside on whichever signals are exposed, pay whatever leaving costs), plus the 2022 figures that put a rented 128-core bare machine at $1,318 a month against roughly $6,055 for the nearest AWS on-demand instance and about $40,000 to buy the equivalent outright. [[Self-Hosting]] states the same trade as the responsibilities a vendor never takes off you, one standing obligation per row: hardware and its depreciation, capacity sized for the peak in advance, upgrade timing and upgrade risk, durability proven by restore drills, and availability with somebody on call. [[Cloud-Native Architecture]] is what a system looks like when it is designed for rented infrastructure instead of lifted onto it, and it is the most heavily audited note here: CNCF's definition is a property list rather than a technology list, so containers, service meshes and microservices are typical ingredients and never membership, and all four claimed advantages of ground-up design trace to papers by the engineers who built the system being measured, with the one independent controlled study finding disaggregated storage alone costing 16.4x on reads and 17.9x on writes. [[Serverless]] pushes metered billing into code execution, and the bill is memory-time rather than time: duration rounded up to the millisecond and multiplied by allocated memory, plus a per-invocation charge, so idle genuinely costs nothing and very many short calls price quite differently from one long one.
- **`Storage/`** where the bytes sit relative to the code that reads them. [[Object Storage]] keeps whole files under names in a flat keyspace and trades most of the POSIX contract for durability nobody administers: no partial write, no general append, no atomic rename and no real directory tree on a general purpose S3 bucket, against eleven nines of designed annual durability, $10^{-11}$ expected loss per object-year, bought by replicating every object across at least three physically separated availability zones that the API never shows you. [[Separation of Storage and Compute]] is the structural property the whole category is organized around, told apart from mere redundancy by the local disk against virtual disk comparison: RAID keeps copies on one machine and loses every one of them with it, a local disk is an ephemeral cache that dies with the instance, and a virtual disk detaches and reattaches, survives a resize, and is replicated by the service that provides it. That separation costs roughly 30x the latency of a local read (about 500 microseconds across a datacenter against about 16 for an SSD) and buys an annual durability figure a local disk has no unit for, since its life ends when the instance does.
- **`Operations/`** who keeps all of the above running. [[DevOps]] is one team accountable for backend services and the infrastructure underneath them, dated to the first devopsdays in Ghent in 2009 and to Allspaw and Hammond's account of deploying Flickr more than ten times a day, and it carries three things at once: the practices the merge brings (automation replacing one-off manual work, ephemeral instances rather than long-lived servers, frequent updates, learning from incidents, and keeping what the organization knows as individual people leave), the distinction from site reliability engineering, which originated at Google in 2003 and is the narrower and more prescriptive of the two rather than a synonym, and the observation that a cloud deployment does not remove operations so much as change its shape, turning capacity planning into financial planning and performance optimization into cost optimization. The customer side of that substitution has since been named and organized as FinOps, a Linux Foundation project since 2020, built on the same move DevOps made with finance standing where operations stood.
